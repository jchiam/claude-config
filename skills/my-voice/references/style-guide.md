# Jonathan's Writing Voice — Style Guide

Derived from five self-authored documents written between 2020 and 2025 (see `samples/`, sanitised and proof-read). Treat this guide as the summary and the samples as ground truth: when they disagree, the samples win.

## 1. Overall register

Plain, measured, institutional-but-human. Writes like an engineering lead explaining a position to management and peers: states the situation, weighs it, then commits to a direction. Not academic, not salesy. Confident without being loud; personality shows up in small, dry asides rather than flourishes.

## 2. Structure

- **Situation before solution.** Typical skeleton: Context → Considerations → Intended Outcomes → Approach → Benefits → Prerequisites. Or: Background → Key Challenges → Observations → Proposal → Drivers → Actionables.
- **Numbered, named findings.** "Observation 1 - Project team lacked cross-functional representation". Later sections cross-reference them ("Related to Observation 2, …").
- **Uneven section lengths are fine.** A heading can hold one sentence ("Interns should be included by default. No additional action items.").
- Headings are plain title case. No emoji, no colons-as-titles.
- Opens directly. At most one plain framing sentence ("This page documents some aspects and initiatives to be considered…").

## 3. Paragraphs and sentences

- Paragraphs of 2–5 sentences. Sentences medium-long, chaining clauses with commas rather than semicolons or dashes.
- Frequently ends a paragraph with a short, flat conclusion: "Product quality ultimately suffered." / "Leave a good impression!"
- **Concessive openers are a signature.** "While X, Y." appears constantly: "While teams benefit from interns' presence…", "While we value diversity in people…", "While actual conditions could be different, …".
- Other habitual sentence openers: "As at writing,", "Given …,", "However,", "As such,", "Ultimately,", "Similarly,", "Regardless of approaches,", "For this round,", "Tactically,", "Ideally,".
- Inverted conditionals: "should they choose to", "should development be required".
- Parentheses for asides, clarifications and status: "(digital transformation)", "(TBC)", "(and product)". **No em-dashes.**

## 4. Word choice

- **Habitual phrases:** "as at writing", "inline with", "said products", "in accordance to", "leverage on", "request for", "in due time", "in future" (no "the"), "at the moment", "to a reasonable extent", "when the need arises", "in a sane manner", "move the needle", "caa <date>".
- Inline "e.g." without surrounding commas: "building blocks laid out by the existing team e.g. tech stack, design system, cloud infra".
- **Acronym-heavy, defined once** in parentheses on first use: "Non-Core Modules (NCM)", "Tech Leads (TLs)". After that, acronym only.
- Concrete organisational nouns: names the specific branch, team, committee or person rather than "stakeholders".
- Occasional colloquial or local touch, used once per piece at most: "mess with", "sell koyok", "don't hire 11 quarterbacks!!", "Ops just run ops".
- Refers to the organisation as "her" ("help the division achieve her vision").
- Comfortable with standard management vocabulary when making the case for a proposal ("plays a key role", "long-term sustainability", "essential"), but grounds it in a specific observation nearby.
- **Spelling:** British (organisation, prioritise, modernisation, programme, recognise).

## 5. Stance and hedging

- Mixes **soft modals for options** ("could be beneficial", "may consider", "can be explored further when the need arises", "The exact format can be deliberated.") with **firm modals for requirements** ("Guardrails must be present", "The team must leverage on…", "must ultimately be executed on the ground").
- States positions plainly when decided: "Product A is no longer a priority for the division's resources." Bolds a single word for emphasis when it matters: "is **not** inline with".
- Voice: "the team proposed", "the division will", "management requested" for organisational facts; "we" for values and principles. Rarely "I".
- Names trade-offs and second-order effects explicitly ("More ARs to manage will increase the cognitive burdens of … which may negatively impact …").

## 6. Lists

- Bullets are plain fragments, usually without terminal full stops, no bold lead-ins.
- Lists are short (2–5 items) and parallel. Prose carries the argument; lists hold enumerations, challenges, prerequisites and options.

## 7. Do NOT write like this (generic AI tells)

- delve, tapestry, pivotal, seamless, robust (as filler), foster, navigate (figurative), "in today's fast-paced…", "a wide array of"
- "It's worth noting that…", "This page will cover several key topics", "Key takeaways", "In conclusion,"
- Em-dashes. Bolded-lead bullets ("**Speed:** …"). Every list forced into threes. "Not only X but also Y" in every paragraph.
- Paragraphs that are all abstraction with no named team, date, number or concrete example. If a sentence would fit any organisation, anchor it to the specific one.
- Hedging stacked on hedging ("may potentially help to possibly…"). Use one modal and move on.

## 8. Before / after

Generic AI draft:

> In today's rapidly evolving landscape, it's worth noting that fostering a culture of collaboration is pivotal. By leveraging cross-functional synergies, teams can seamlessly navigate complex challenges — ultimately delivering robust, high-quality outcomes that delight stakeholders.

In Jonathan's voice:

> While each squad delivers features well on its own, issues tend to fall through the cracks between dev, QA and ops. Production bugs get thrown back to the delivery track, and fixes are often superficial. The team should move towards shared ownership of quality, starting with a small squad that owns a feature end-to-end, including its operations.

## 9. Samples

The samples have been sanitised (organisations, systems, people and dates replaced with neutral placeholders such as "the division", "Platform A", "Branch X", "@Lead A") and proof-read for typos and grammar. When writing for real, use the actual names the user provides; do not carry the placeholders over. Keep the idioms ("inline with", "leverage on", "in accordance to", "as at writing"); they are part of the voice, not errors to be corrected.
